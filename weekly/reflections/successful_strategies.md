# Successful optimisation strategies — reflection

## The strategies behind my strongest results

Four strategies account for nearly all of my results, and each earned its place by evidence rather than by design.

**Classifying functions by evidence state, and matching the strategy to the state.** From mid-project onward, every function was placed each week into one of four states — verified, improving, localising, or no reliable signal — with a defined action for each. This mattered because the eight functions turned out to be fundamentally different problems wearing the same interface: one had a sharp corner maximum, two had forgiving plateaus, one was almost entirely flat, three turned out to be noisy. A single strategy applied uniformly performed poorly in the opening rounds for exactly this reason. The classification influenced everything downstream: it decided where the weekly budget went, how bold each query could be, and when a function stopped receiving attention.

**A reliability check before any model was believed.** From week 5, each function's model was tested weekly by withholding every known point in turn and asking the model to predict it; any model that predicted worse than a simple average was barred from directing queries. This was the highest-impact change of the project. It exposed that three of my eight models were actively misleading, and it explains the before-and-after pattern in my results: every damaging decision (weeks 4, 6 and 7) came from acting on an unvalidated prediction; almost every gain after week 8 came once the gate was in place.

**Small steps near confirmed results.** Eleven of my new bests came from queries placed within 0.02 of an existing best — including the improvements on functions 6 and 7 in weeks 8 and 11, and function 2's only improvement of the project. The effectiveness has a simple cause: at 20–50 data points per function, models interpolate well and extrapolate badly, so the reliable information is concentrated near observed points. Once the pattern was visible in the data, it changed my decision rule permanently: no query was placed more than a small step from evidence unless its purpose was explicitly informational.

**Deliberate verification queries.** I spent queries re-submitting known coordinates — three reproduction tests — which could never improve a score. They were among the best queries of the project. They established which results were bankable, they converted the final submission from a theory into a guarantee for the deterministic functions, and in the final round they revealed that three functions return different values at identical inputs, which reframes what their "best observed" values actually mean.

Two individual decisions deserve mention alongside the systematic strategies: the week 2 corner submission on function 5 (combining the brief's "single peak" description with the model's boundary-pointing behaviour; 1,089 → 8,662, the largest single gain of the project) and the closing decision on function 1 to take one further step along a rising signal rather than banking the guaranteed value — which returned 53% more than the banked figure.

## What defines a successful strategy

Outcomes are necessary but not sufficient. My view after thirteen rounds is that a successful strategy has four properties, and the leaderboard only measures the first.

**Outcomes**, obviously — six of eight functions materially improved is the headline. But **adaptability** is what produced those outcomes: my strategy in week 12 shared almost nothing with week 1 except the core loop, and every change was a response to observed results rather than a plan made in advance. **Reasoning quality** matters independently of results, because the sample sizes are too small to distinguish luck from skill by outcomes alone — the corner gamble on function 5 succeeded and the neural-network gamble in week 4 failed, but what actually separates them is that one combined two independent sources of evidence and the other rested on a single unvalidated model. And **verifiability**: a result you can reproduce on demand is worth more than a higher number observed once. The final round proved this concretely — the "best observed" values on my three noisy functions were partly luck, and only the verification queries revealed which was which.

**Efficiency** binds these together: with thirteen queries per function in total, the real measure of any strategy is what it returns per query spent — including queries spent on information rather than score.

## Application beyond the capstone

The transfer to professional ML work is direct, because the capstone's structure — expensive evaluations, limited budget, incomplete information — is the structure of most applied data science. In my field (media: content recommendation, subscriber retention, advertising placement), the equivalents are exact. An audience test costs real exposure and real weeks, the way a portal query cost a round. The strategies carry over as operating practices: validate a model against held-out data before acting on its scores (a churn model's confidence means nothing until checked against known churners); keep a verified fallback before experimenting (never test a new ad-load policy without the proven one ready to restore); distinguish deterministic from noisy systems before trusting single measurements (one good week for a test variant is not a result — a lesson my three noisy functions taught late); and log every decision with its reasoning, because in a long campaign the record of *why* is what converts individual results into an improving strategy.

## Peer strategies — what worked, and where we overlap

The peer whose work most influenced mine was **Omkar Joshi**. Two of his practices became part of my toolkit. His leave-one-out R² diagnostic — checking whether each function's model could predict held-out points — became the reliability check that anchored my whole second half; he had reported negative R² on several of his functions, I ran the same test and found the same pattern on mine (three models predicting worse than the average), and my strategy changed permanently that week. His second practice I adopted was consensus across independent measures: he ranked input importance using four separate methods and only acted where they agreed. I implemented a three-method version (model lengthscales, network gradients, random-forest importance) on my eight-input function, and it correctly identified the two inputs that carry no signal. What made both practices effective is the same underlying principle: no single model or metric is trusted on its own account.

One of his suggestions also gave me a useful negative result: his observation that a high value of one particular input had worked for his function 8. I tested the equivalent on mine in week 7 and it failed clearly (9.03 against a 9.95 incumbent).

**Dr Matthew Harper's** approach was effective for a complementary reason: rather than trusting one model, he ran ensembles — multiple Gaussian Process configurations selected by cross-validation, a hybrid of acquisition scores, and a neural-network candidate generated independently as a cross-check, acting with most confidence where the methods agreed. **Loukia Kritioti** did something similar at the acquisition level, comparing the suggestions of several scoring rules and reading agreement between them as a signal. Both are consensus strategies; my single-model-plus-trust-gate approach reached similar decisions with less engineering, which I'd argue was better suited to this data scale — but their versions scale more naturally to larger problems where one model genuinely can't cover the space.

## Suggestions, and what my peers changed in my thinking

Two suggestions I'd offer. First, to those running ensemble and consensus approaches: add deliberate reproduction queries. Consensus between models can tell you a prediction is stable, but only re-querying the same point can tell you whether the *function* is stable — and my final round showed that distinction carries real consequences for which results are bankable. Second, a repository observation from exploring peers' GitHub work: documented code is helpful, but documented decisions — which rule was chosen each week, why, and what happened is more valuable.

What my peers most broadened was my definition of success itself. Yiqun Huang made the point mid-course that a query can be valuable for what it teaches even when the score doesn't move, and judged his weeks partly on information gained; I ended the project scoring my own rounds the same way — several of my best queries produced no score improvement at all, and my function 1 result exists only because eleven scoreless rounds were treated as information rather than failure. Between his framing, Omkar's diagnostics and the ensemble-builders' distrust of single models, my process and end results were definitely improved.
